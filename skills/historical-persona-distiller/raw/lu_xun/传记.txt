鲁迅 (Lu Xun, Zhou Shuren, 1881-1936)

来源：Wikipedia (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lu_Xun)

---

Lu Xun (魯迅), pen name of Zhou Shuren (周树人), was born Zhou Zhangshou on 25 September 1881 in Shaoxing, Zhejiang, into a declining family of landlords and scholar-officials. His paternal grandfather, Zhou Fuqing, had achieved the prestigious rank of Hanlin Academician, but the family's fortunes had already begun to wane by the time of Lu Xun's birth. His father, Zhou Boyi, was stripped of his government position in 1893 for attempting to bribe an examination official, and the resulting scandal led to the grandfather's imprisonment until 1901. The father subsequently fell into opium addiction and heavy drinking, dying of an asthma attack in 1896, possibly complicated by dropsy. Lu Xun's mother, though unschooled, taught herself to read and write.

His early education centered on the Confucian classics, though he grew drawn to folk stories and the mythological narratives of the Classic of Mountains and Seas. After abandoning the imperial examination path in 1898, he attended the Jiangnan Naval Academy in Nanjing, then the School of Mines and Railways, graduating in 1902. He went to Japan in 1902, and enrolled at Sendai Medical School in 1904, where a pivotal lantern slide depicting the apathy of Chinese onlookers at a public execution during the Russo-Japanese War prompted him to abandon medicine for literature. As he later wrote in his preface to Nahan, he resolved to become a "literary physician" to China's spiritual illness rather than treat its bodies.

Lu Xun returned to China around 1909, holding teaching positions before securing employment at the Ministry of Education of the Beiyang government following the 1911 Xinhai Revolution. In 1917, he accepted Qian Xuantong's invitation to write for the journal La Jeunesse (New Youth), and in April 1918 published Diary of a Madman (狂人日记), the first modern Chinese novel written in vernacular language. He followed this with The True Story of Ah Q (阿Q正传, 1921), and collected his early stories in the volumes Nahan (Call to Arms, 1923) and Panghuang (Wandering, 1926). He also wrote prose poems collected as Wild Grass (野草, 1927) and autobiographical essays gathered in Dawn Blossoms Plucked at Dusk (朝花夕拾, 1932).

His marriage to Zhu An, arranged by his mother in 1906, was never romantic, though he supported her financially throughout his life. A falling out with his younger brother Zhou Zuoren, also a noted writer, occurred in 1923. Around 1925, he began a romantic relationship with his student Xu Guangping, with whom he had a son, Zhou Haiying, born in 1929. After teaching at Xiamen University and Sun Yat-sen University, he settled in Shanghai by late 1927, where he became co-founder of the League of Left-Wing Writers in 1930. He engaged increasingly with Marxist thought while maintaining an uneasy distance from the Chinese Communist Party, reportedly telling his student Feng Xuefeng that he would be among the first killed should the revolution succeed.

In declining health from tuberculosis through the 1930s, Lu Xun died of bronchial asthma on 19 October 1936 in Shanghai. Mao Zedong later pronounced him "the saint of modern China," though Mao privately remarked that had Lu Xun lived, he would have "either remained silent or been imprisoned."
