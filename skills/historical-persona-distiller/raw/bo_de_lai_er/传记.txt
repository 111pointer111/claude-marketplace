Charles-Pierre Baudelaire (1821-1867)

来源：Wikipedia (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Baudelaire)

---

Charles-Pierre Baudelaire was born on 9 April 1821 in Paris and baptized two months later at the Saint-Sulpice Catholic church. His father, Joseph-François Baudelaire, a senior civil servant and amateur artist, was thirty-four years older than his mother Caroline (née Dufaÿs), Joseph-François's second wife. Following his father's death in February 1827, Caroline married Lieutenant Colonel Jacques Aupick, who later rose to become a French ambassador. Baudelaire's biographers have often interpreted this rupture with his mother as a formative trauma shaping his later excesses.

Educated initially in Lyon and later at the Lycée Louis-le-Grand in Paris, where he studied law, Baudelaire frequented prostitutes and may have contracted gonorrhea and syphilis during this period while running up debts largely for clothing. Upon completing his degree in 1839, he rejected a career in law or diplomacy in favor of literature. His stepfather dispatched him on a voyage to Calcutta in 1841 intended to curb his dissolute habits; though he never reached India, the journey through Mauritius and Réunion left lasting impressions later transmuted into his poetry. Returning to Paris, he composed poems of Les Fleurs du mal and received a sizable inheritance at twenty-one that he rapidly squandered, prompting his family to place his property in trust. He became celebrated in artistic circles as a dandy and free-spender, took the actress Jeanne Duval as his mistress around 1842, and made a suicide attempt. He briefly participated in the Revolutions of 1848 and contributed to a revolutionary newspaper.

His publishing career commenced with the art review "Salon of 1845," published under the pseudonym Baudelaire Dufaÿs, which gained notice for championing Eugène Delacroix. After further Salon reviews and his novella La Fanfarlo (1847), he published Les Fleurs du mal in 1857, a volume treating themes of sex, death, lesbianism, metamorphosis, and the corruption of the city that scandalized mainstream critics. He, his publisher Auguste Poulet-Malassis, and his printer were successfully prosecuted for offending public morals; six poems were suppressed and later printed in Brussels as Les Épaves (1866), while a revised edition appeared in 1861. On 11 May 1949, nearly a century later, the judgment was officially reversed and the banned poems reinstated in France.

Baudelaire produced translations of Edgar Allan Poe's works between 1847 and 1865, published as Histoires extraordinaires (1856), Nouvelles histoires extraordinaires (1857), and Histoires grotesques et sérieuses (1865). His essay Le Peintre de la vie moderne (1863) articulated the concept of modernity and the flâneur, the observer of urban life. Among his other notable works are Le Spleen de Paris (Petits Poèmes en prose), Les Paradis artificiels (1860), and the posthumously published journals Mon cœur mis à nu and Fusées. His long-term use of laudanum and, in Brussels, excessive drinking compounded the effects of poverty and illness. After his stepfather's death in 1857 he sought reconciliation with his mother, who finally permitted him to live with her at Honfleur. His relationship with Jeanne Duval endured on and off throughout his life; he also maintained relations with the actress Marie Daubrun and the courtesan Apollonie Sabatier.

In 1864 Baudelaire departed for Belgium hoping to sell his works and deliver lectures. There he suffered a massive stroke in 1866, followed by paralysis and aphasia. After more than a year of incapacitation, he received the last rites of the Catholic Church and died in Paris on 31 August 1867, aged forty-six. His funeral at the Saint-Honoré d'Eylau church drew only a few dozen mourners, and he was interred at the Cimetière du Montparnasse. His mother Caroline, who paid off his substantial debts after his death, outlived him by nearly four years, dying on 16 August 1871.
