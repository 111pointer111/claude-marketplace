Niels Henrik Abel (5 August 1802 – 6 April 1829)

来源：Wikipedia (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Henrik_Abel)

---

Niels Henrik Abel was born on 5 August 1802 in Nedstrand, Denmark–Norway, as the second child of the pastor Søren Georg Abel and Anne Marie Simonsen. His father held a degree in theology and philosophy, and the Abel family had originated in Schleswig before coming to Norway in the seventeenth century. His mother came from Risør and was said to be the daughter of the richest person in that town. Abel entered the Cathedral School in Christiania in 1815 at the age of thirteen, where a new mathematics teacher named Bernt Michael Holmboe recognized his talent and gave him private lessons. By the time he entered the Royal Frederick University in 1821, Abel was already the most knowledgeable mathematician in Norway, and he graduated in 1822 with exceptional marks in mathematics.

During his university years, Abel began working on the quintic equation, which had puzzled mathematicians for over 250 years. After initially believing he had solved it, he discovered a mistake in 1823 that led him to prove there is no general algebraic solution for equations of degree five or higher, a result now known as the Abel–Ruffini theorem. He published this proof in 1824 in a concise French memoir and a more detailed version appeared in 1826 in the first volume of Crelle's Journal.

In 1825, Abel traveled abroad with university friends, spending four months in Berlin where he became closely associated with August Leopold Crelle and contributed seven articles to the first volume of the newly founded Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik. He continued to Freiberg for research on elliptic and Abelian functions, then journeyed to Paris in July 1826. There he submitted what he considered his most important work on the addition of algebraic differentials to the French Academy of Sciences, but Augustin-Louis Cauchy set it aside and it was forgotten until after Abel's death. His modesty prevented him from promoting his research in Paris, and Adrien-Marie Legendre, who later called his work on elliptic functions "a monument more lasting than bronze," praised him only after the fact. Abel returned to Norway in May 1827 with finances exhausted and his scholarship not renewed.

Abel made many lasting contributions to mathematics, including a rigorous proof of the binomial theorem valid for all numbers as a sixteen-year-old, fundamental work on elliptic integrals and elliptic functions revealing their double periodicity, the foundations of Abelian functions, Abel's theorem on the continuity of power series, Abel's summation formula, Abel's test, and the concept of Abelian groups, a term that has become so common it is conventionally written with a lowercase "a." Regarding his work, the French mathematician Charles Hermite remarked, "Abel has left mathematicians enough to keep them busy for five hundred years."

While in Paris Abel had contracted tuberculosis. At Christmas 1828 he traveled by sled to Froland, Norway, to visit his fiancée Christine Kemp, becoming seriously ill on the journey. He died on 6 April 1829 at the age of twenty-six, just two days before a letter arrived from August Leopold Crelle informing him of an appointment as professor at the University of Berlin. His legacy endures in the Abel Prize in mathematics, first awarded in 2003, and in the many mathematical concepts that bear his name.
